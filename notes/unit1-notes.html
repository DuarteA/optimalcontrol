1 Optimal control

Optimality is a useful design principle in many engineering contexts: we size a refrigerator to minimize its energy use, tune an engine to minimize a car’s fuel consumption, shape an aircraft wing to minimize drag, and schedule elevators in a building to minimize passenger waiting time. It is also a principle nature itself seems to follow – many physical laws can be derived from optimality arguments – and, arguably, a principle we apply informally every day whenever we weigh a decision.

Optimal control gives this idea a precise mathematical form for a broad class of problems. These are problems in which optimal decisions, or control actions, are chosen over a period of time so as to minimize a cost that reflects a final objective and, often, intermediate objectives along the way. Optimal control problems arise in the control of physical systems – mechanical, electrical, biological – but also in economics, computer science, game theory, and many other fields. Fig. 1 gives a sense of this breadth, from robotics to logistics to biology.

It is worth contrasting optimal control with the more familiar setting of static optimization, which asks for a single optimal decision – for instance, the price of a product, or the slope of a line that best fits a data set. Optimal control differs from static optimization in two important respects. First, it asks for several optimal decisions, made sequentially over time. Second, because the system’s future state cannot be predicted with certainty, a good decision at each point in time should be allowed to depend on the state actually observed at that point, rather than being fixed in advance; that is, the decisions form a control law that can react to disturbances. Given the current state, previous decisions are irrelevant to determining future decisions. This gives optimal control problems a special structure, which the solution methods developed later in the course exploit. Driving a car around a race track, positioning the tip of a robot arm despite external disturbances, or playing chess are all optimal control problems in this sense: not a single choice, but a sequence of choices, each informed by how the situation has evolved.

1.1 Formulating an optimal control problem

Every optimal control problem in this course is built from two ingredients: a dynamic model and a cost function.

The dynamic model specifies the rules of the problem, or the governing equations of the physical system under control. Central to it is the notion of state: a summary of all the information relevant to making future decisions. Control actions are the decisions available to the controller at each point in time; they influence how the state evolves. That evolution may be deterministic, or it may be stochastic, driven by disturbances outside the controller’s influence.

The cost function encapsulates the goals of the problem. It is typically additive over time – a sum of costs incurred at each decision stage – and, by convention, the objective is to minimize it (a reward to be maximized can always be recast as a cost to be minimized by negating it).

The goal of the problem is then to find a control policy minimizing the cost: a policy being a set of functions, one per time instant, mapping the current state to an action. A closely related, and often easier, question is to compute a single optimal path – a sequence of optimal decisions over time – for one specific, given initial condition, rather than a policy valid for every possible initial condition. (In continuous-state settings, introduced shortly, the same idea is usually called an optimal trajectory instead.)

1.2 Classes of optimal control problems and applications

The course begins with problems where the state and action spaces belong to discrete spaces (e.g. \(\{1,2,\dots,n\}\)), and later moves to continuous spaces (\(\mathbb{R}^n\)). Time, throughout the course, is always taken to be discrete; continuous-time decision problems will appear occasionally, but whenever they do, we will convert them into stage-decision or discrete optimization problems rather than treating continuous time directly.

When a discrete- or continuous-state problem is additionally subject to stochastic disturbances, it is called a Markov Decision Process (MDP). This gives four broad classes of problems that recur throughout the course:

State space Stochastic disturbances
Discrete decision problems Discrete No
Discrete MDPs Discrete Yes
Continuous decision/control problems Continuous No

The sections that follow make several of these problems concrete, since they will recur as running examples through the course – including settings where uncertainty is explicitly present.

1.2.1 Discrete decision problems

Discrete decision problems and discrete MDPs arise naturally in operational research, management, and finance, where optimal control ideas govern inventory control, the control of a queue, or the control of data and traffic networks. In computer science, closely related problems include finding shortest paths in graphs, scheduling, games, and selection problems, among other applications.

A discrete decision problem is naturally specified by a transition diagram with \(h-1\) decision stages: circles represent the possible states at each stage, arrows represent the actions that lead from a state at one stage to a state at the next, and each arrow carries a cost \(c^k_{i,j}\), the cost of taking action \(j\) from state \(i\) at stage \(k\); at the terminal stage \(h\), each state \(i\) carries a terminal cost \(c^h_i\).

Working with such diagrams typically raises two challenges, illustrated below with the small example of Fig. 3 (a): (i) determining an optimal path for a given initial state, minimizing the sum of costs incurred at every stage, including the terminal stage (b); and (ii) determining an optimal policy that specifies, for every state, the first decision of the optimal path from that state to the terminal stage (c). Together, these motivate the need for a systematic algorithm, rather than exhaustive enumeration of every path.

A first running example throughout the course is inventory control: how should a shop manage the supply of a product over time? Overstocking is costly – it ties up physical space and risks technological obsolescence – while understocking undermines sales; the optimal policy must balance the two (Fig. 5 (a), below).

A second is shortest paths in graphs: given a road network, what is the shortest route from one city to another – for instance, from Bucharest to Lugoj in the map below?

1.2.2 Continuous decision problems

The discrete framework generalizes naturally to problems with a general state and input space, such as \(\mathbb{R}^n\). A continuous decision problem is described by a dynamic model \[x_{k+1} = f_k(x_k,u_k), \qquad k\in\{0,\dots,h-1\},\] where \(x_k\) is the state and \(u_k\) the control input at time \(k\), together with a cost function \[\sum_{k=0}^{h-1} g_k(x_k,u_k) + g_h(x_h).\] As before, there are two related goals: (i) find a policy \(\pi=\{\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{h-1}\}\), with \(u_k=\mu_k(x_k)\), that achieves the minimum cost for every initial condition, or (ii) find a single trajectory \(\{(x_0,u_0),(x_1,u_1),\dots,(x_{h-1},u_{h-1})\}\) achieving the minimum cost for a given initial condition.

The most prominent application of this framework is digital control: how should one design a digital controller for a physical system, complete with digital-to-analog and analog-to-digital converters, sensors, and actuators? Variants of this problem include cases where the full state is measured versus only a partial output, and cases with or without external disturbances.

Several concrete case studies illustrate the range of this framework. Mixing two fluids in minimum time can be posed as a digital control problem in which a camera observes the mixture and an actuator applies one of four possible rotations once every \(h\) seconds.

Digitally controlling a unicycle robot – subject to constraints on its speed and rotation rate – to complete a maneuver in minimum time is another instance (Fig. 9 (a), below). So is flying a quadcopter while taking wind-preview information into account (Fig. 9 (b)); moving a satellite between orbits with minimum energy or fuel expenditure – an important concern given the limited fuel satellites carry (Fig. 9 (c)); and moving the tip of a robot arm from one configuration to another in minimum time (Fig. 9 (d)).

1.2.3 Continuous-time optimal control problems

Occasionally the underlying physical system is more naturally described in continuous time, via a dynamic model \[\dot{x}(t) = f(t,x(t),u(t)), \qquad x(0)=x_0, \quad t\in[0,h],\] and a cost function \[\int_0^h g(t,x(t),u(t))\,dt + g_h(x(h)),\] with the same two goals as before: find a feedback policy minimizing the cost for every initial condition, or find a single control input minimizing the cost.

Most control-systems applications are naturally posed this way: in traditional process control, they arise in stabilizing and regulating systems such as an inverted pendulum, a mass-spring damper, a double integrator, or a quadcopter; in aerospace, a canonical problem is the minimum-fuel launch of a satellite. As noted above, whenever such problems arise in this course we will approximate them as discrete-time decision or optimal control problems, which is the setting the dynamic programming algorithm developed below is built for.

1.2.4 The reinforcement learning setting

A final, important variation on this theme arises when no dynamic model is available at all – only the real system itself, or a simulator that can be run, is accessible. This is the reinforcement learning (RL) setting.

Even though no explicit dynamic model is given, a model of the form described above is still implicitly present for analysis purposes; the difference is that it is unknown to the decision-maker. A reward or cost function still encapsulates the goals of the problem, but it is now obtained through the agent’s interaction with the environment rather than computed from a known model. This is, in fact, the key distinguishing feature of reinforcement learning relative to the classical dynamic programming setting introduced above: the transition dynamics are unknown and must be learned from interaction, rather than given. The goal becomes to find a control policy – again, a function from states to actions – that minimizes cost based on data gathered through trial and error.

Two examples illustrate this setting. Learning the optimal policy for a ghost chasing Pacman – deciding, at each step, whether to move up, down, left, or right so as to catch Pacman in minimum time – is a natural discrete RL problem.

Learning to control a robot arm to perform a ball-balancing task is a continuous analogue.

2 The dynamic programming algorithm

Having introduced the range of problems optimal control addresses, we turn to the first general-purpose method for solving them: dynamic programming (DP). Dynamic programming allows us to find not just a single optimal path, but entire policies – functions mapping states to actions – which can then be used to control a system in the presence of disturbances. It can also be used to compute optimal paths or trajectories directly, although, as we shall see later in the course, other methods can be more efficient for that narrower task.

2.1 The principle of optimality

Dynamic programming rests on a simple but powerful observation, known as the principle of optimality. Consider a simple example: the shortest route from Eindhoven to Paris happens to pass through Antwerp. The principle of optimality then tells us that the portion of that route from Antwerp to Paris must itself be the shortest route between those two cities – if a shorter route from Antwerp to Paris existed, splicing it in would produce a shorter Eindhoven-to-Paris route, contradicting the optimality of the original route.

Stated more generally, for a discrete optimization problem: the tail of an optimal path is also optimal. Given an optimal path from stage \(0\) to stage \(h\), consider the state at some intermediate stage \(j\) that lies on this optimal path. Then the decisions along the optimal path from stage \(j\) onward to stage \(h\) are themselves optimal for the subproblem that starts at that state at stage \(j\).

The principle of optimality is not specific to discrete optimization problems – it applies equally to stage-decision problems and to continuous-time optimal control problems – and it is the foundation on which the dynamic programming algorithm is built.

The argument sketched informally above for the Eindhoven-Antwerp-Paris example is worked out precisely, by contradiction, in the following exercise.

TipExercise: prove the principle of optimality

Consider an optimal path from stage \(0\) to stage \(h\), and an intermediate stage \(j\) that lies on this path. Prove, by contradiction, that the portion of the path from stage \(j\) onward to stage \(h\) is itself optimal for the subproblem that starts at that state at stage \(j\).

NoteSolution

Split the cost of the optimal path into the cost of the portion before stage \(j\) and the cost of the portion from stage \(j\) onward: \[\text{cost} = \text{cost}_{[0,j)} + \text{cost}_{[j,h]}.\]

Now suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that the portion of this path from stage \(j\) to stage \(h\) is not optimal for the subproblem starting at that state at stage \(j\) – that is, there exists an alternative continuation with strictly smaller cost, \[\underline{\text{cost}}_{[j,h]} < \text{cost}_{[j,h]}.\]

Splicing this better continuation onto the original path’s first portion then produces a path from stage \(0\) to stage \(h\) with strictly smaller total cost, \[\text{cost}_{[0,j)} + \underline{\text{cost}}_{[j,h]} < \text{cost}_{[0,j)} + \text{cost}_{[j,h]},\] contradicting the assumption that the original path was optimal. Hence the tail of an optimal path must itself be optimal.

2.2 The algorithm

The principle of optimality suggests a natural algorithm for discrete decision problems: rather than search forward from stage \(0\), compute optimal decisions backward from the final stage. Concretely, the dynamic programming algorithm proceeds as follows.

  1. Start at the final decision stage \(h\), and set the cost-to-go at stage \(h\) equal to the terminal cost.
  2. For every state at stage \(k<h\), compute the optimal action by minimizing the sum of the immediate cost and the cost-to-go of the resulting next state; call the minimum value the cost-to-go at stage \(k\) for that state.
  3. Repeat step 2, moving backward through \(k=h-1,h-2,\dots,0\).

Because each state at each stage only ever needs to look one stage ahead (at the cost-to-go values already computed), the entire diagram can be swept once, backward, rather than requiring an exhaustive search over all possible forward paths.

2.3 A worked example

Fig. 19 shows the algorithm being applied stage by stage, backward through a small transition diagram.

Once the backward sweep is complete, both the optimal policy and the optimal paths can be read directly off the diagram.

While running the algorithm, a decision is made at every state and every stage in order to compute that state’s cost-to-go; this decision is precisely the decision specified by the optimal policy at that state/stage. To obtain the optimal path for a given initial state, simply follow the arrows selected by the policy from that initial state through to the final stage. The cost-to-go computed at stage \(0\) for that initial state coincides exactly with the total cost of the resulting optimal path.

TipQuick exercise: a two-stage recursion by hand

Consider a discrete decision problem with \(h=2\) stages, states \(x_0\in\{1,2\}\) at stage \(0\), states \(x_1\in\{1,2\}\) at stage \(1\), and a single terminal state \(x_2\). The stage costs are: from \(x_0=1\), cost \(3\) to reach \(x_1=1\) and cost \(5\) to reach \(x_1=2\); from \(x_0=2\), cost \(1\) to reach \(x_1=1\) and cost \(4\) to reach \(x_1=2\); from \(x_1=1\), cost \(2\) to reach \(x_2\); from \(x_1=2\), cost \(6\) to reach \(x_2\). The terminal cost is \(g_2(x_2)=0\).

  1. Compute the cost-to-go \(J_1(x_1)\) for each \(x_1\in\{1,2\}\).

  2. Compute the cost-to-go \(J_0(x_0)\) for each \(x_0\in\{1,2\}\), and give the optimal decision at each state.

NoteSolution

  1. At stage \(1\) there is only one available action from each state, so \(J_1(1) = 2\) and \(J_1(2) = 6\).

  2. At stage \(0\): \[J_0(1) = \min\{3+J_1(1),\ 5+J_1(2)\} = \min\{3+2,\ 5+6\} = \min\{5,11\} = 5,\] achieved by moving to \(x_1=1\); and \[J_0(2) = \min\{1+J_1(1),\ 4+J_1(2)\} = \min\{1+2,\ 4+6\} = \min\{3,10\} = 3,\] also achieved by moving to \(x_1=1\). So the optimal policy sends both \(x_0=1\) and \(x_0=2\) to \(x_1=1\) at stage \(0\), and from \(x_1=1\) (the only option) to \(x_2\) at stage \(1\). The optimal cost-to-go from \(x_0=1\) is \(5\); from \(x_0=2\) it is \(3\).

# describing the actions available from that state. Add/remove stages,
# states, or actions freely -- the recursion below adapts automatically.
cost = {
    0: {1: {1: 3, 2: 5}, 2: {1: 1, 2: 4}},
    1: {1: {"end": 2}, 2: {"end": 6}},
}
# terminal_cost maps each state at the final stage to its terminal cost g_h
terminal_cost = {"end": 0}
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------

# --- backward recursion (no need to edit below this line) ------------------
stages = sorted(cost.keys())      # decision stages, e.g. [0, 1, ...]
h = stages[-1] + 1                 # terminal stage index

J = {h: terminal_cost}             # cost-to-go, keyed by stage then state
policy = {}                        # optimal next state, keyed by stage then state

for k in reversed(stages):
    J[k], policy[k] = {}, {}

        best_next, arc_cost = min(

        )
        J[k][state] = arc_cost + J[k + 1][best_next]
        policy[k][state] = best_next

for k in stages:
    for state in sorted(J[k], key=str):
        print(f"stage {k}, state {state}: J = {J[k][state]}, "
              f"optimal next state = {policy[k][state]}")

2.4 Non-uniqueness

It can happen that, at some state and stage, more than one action achieves the same minimum cost-to-go. When this occurs, any of the tied options may be selected; the algorithm simply picks one of them (arbitrarily, or by convention) and proceeds. The result is a single optimal policy, even though, in general, several different optimal policies may exist – all of them achieving the same optimal cost-to-go, but potentially prescribing different actions at the states and stages where a tie occurred.

TipQuick exercise: counting optimal policies under ties

  1. Suppose that, while running the DP algorithm, a tie in the cost-to-go between two actions occurs at \(r\) different state-stage pairs (possibly at different stages). How many optimal policies does the problem have?

  2. Suppose instead that ties occur at only two state-stage pairs, with \(3\) optimal actions available at the first and \(4\) optimal actions available at the second. How many optimal policies are there in total?

NoteSolution

  1. At each of the \(r\) tied state-stage pairs, either of the two optimal actions can be selected independently of the choices made at the other tied pairs, and every such combination yields a distinct optimal policy (all achieving the same optimal cost). There are therefore \(2^r\) optimal policies.

  2. By the same reasoning, the choice made at the first state-stage pair (among \(3\) optimal actions) is independent of the choice made at the second (among \(4\) optimal actions), so the total number of optimal policies is \(3 \times 4 = 12\).

2.5 Inventory control, revisited

We now return to the inventory control problem introduced earlier and formulate it precisely, as a running example for the dynamic programming algorithm. The problem is to control the supply of a single product over time. The dynamic model is \[x_{k+1} = \max\{x_k+u_k-d_k,0\}, \qquad u_k \in \{0,1,\dots,N-x_k\},\] where \(x_k\) is the number of items in stock, \(u_k\) the supply ordered at stage \(k\), \(d_k\) the demand at stage \(k\), and \(N\) the storage capacity. The cost function is \[\sum_{k=0}^{h-1} g_k(x_k,u_k) + g_h(x_h),\] where the stage cost is \[g_k(x_k,u_k) = \big(c_1(x_k) + c\,u_k + c_{tr}\lVert u_k\rVert_0\big) - p\min\{d_k,x_k+u_k\},\] with \(\lVert u_k\rVert_0\) equal to \(0\) if \(u_k=0\) and \(1\) otherwise, \(g_h\) the terminal cost, \(p\) the selling price per item, \(c\) the purchase price per item, \(c_{tr}\) the (fixed, per-order) transportation price, and \(c_1\) the storage cost.

The corresponding transition diagram represents, at each stage, the possible numbers of items in stock as states, with the chosen supplies determining the transitions between them.

To make this concrete, consider a numerical instance with \(h=4\) stages and capacity \(N=4\), demand \(d_0=d_1=2\), \(d_2=d_3=1\), selling price \(p=10\), purchase price \(c=5\), transportation price \(c_{tr}=0.5\), storage cost \(c_1(i)=0.2i\) for \(i\in\{0,\dots,N\}\), and terminal cost \(g_4(i)=-r_{i+1}\) for \(i\in\{0,\dots,4\}\), with \(r=[0\ 4.8\ 9.6\ 14.4\ 19.2]\). Applying the backward recursion to this instance proceeds exactly as in the abstract worked example above.

Once the recursion is complete, the resulting policy and the optimal path for each possible initial state can be read off the diagram, exactly as described above.

A full Python implementation of this example – reproducing the same numbers, including the open-loop/closed-loop figures further below in this section – is available in code/unit1/inventory_control.py, built on top of the general solver in code/unit1/dp.py; the original annotated notebooks are also included there (A_1_inventorycontrol.ipynb, A_1_dp.ipynb). 2.6 Coping with uncertainty

NoteOptional: coping with uncertainty

The dynamic programming algorithm developed above assumes the demand sequence \(d_k\) is known in advance. In practice, of course, it is not: the real value of \(d_k\) only becomes known once stage \(k\) is reached. This section uses the inventory control example to illustrate the difference between planning that ignores this uncertainty and planning that exploits it – a distinction that motivates the extension to stochastic dynamic programming taken up in the next unit.

Consider again the inventory control example, and suppose the plan was computed assuming an expected demand of \(d_2=1\) at stage \(2\), but the realized demand turns out to be \(d_2=2\) instead.

Because the realized demand differs from what was planned for, the state actually reached at stage \(3\) is \(x_3=0\) rather than the \(x_3=1\) that had been anticipated. Two ways of proceeding from here are worth distinguishing:

  • Open loop: ignore the discrepancy and blindly apply the action \(u_3=0\) that had originally been planned for the anticipated state.
  • Closed loop: instead, use the dynamic programming policy computed for the state actually observed, \(x_3=0\), which prescribes \(u_3=1\).

The two choices lead to different realized costs. For the open-loop choice, \[g_0(0,4)+g_1(2,0)+g_2(0,2)+g_3(0,0)+g_4(0) = 0.5 - 19.6 + (-9.5) + 0 + 0 = -28.6,\] whereas for the closed-loop choice, \[g_0(0,4)+g_1(2,0)+g_2(0,2)+g_3(0,1)+g_4(0) = 0.5 - 19.6 + (-9.5) + (-4.5) + 0 = -33.1.\] The closed-loop cost is lower (recall costs here are net of revenue, so more negative is better): by reacting to the observed state rather than following a fixed plan, the closed-loop policy recovers some of the value lost to the unexpected demand.

The same comparison can be made in expectation. If \(\text{Prob}[d_2=1]=0.5\) and \(\text{Prob}[d_2=2]=0.5\), and the open-loop and closed-loop costs when \(d_2=1\) both equal \(-28.4\) (the case that matches what was planned for), then the expected costs are \[\text{Open loop:} \quad 0.5\times(-28.4) + 0.5\times(-28.6) = -28.5,\] \[\text{Closed loop:} \quad 0.5\times(-28.4) + 0.5\times(-33.1) = -30.75.\] The closed-loop policy achieves a strictly lower expected cost, since it is able to adapt its later decisions to the disturbance actually realized, rather than committing to a fixed sequence of actions in advance. Making this idea precise – computing policies that directly minimize expected cost under a known probability distribution over disturbances – is exactly the subject of Unit 2, stochastic dynamic programming.

TipQuick exercise: open loop vs. closed loop without disturbances

True or false: if a discrete decision problem has no disturbances at all (the dynamic model \(x_{k+1}=f_k(x_k,u_k)\) is fully deterministic), then the open-loop and closed-loop strategies always achieve exactly the same cost, for any initial state. Justify your answer.

NoteSolution

True. The gap between open-loop and closed-loop only arises because a disturbance can push the realized state away from the state that was anticipated when the plan was made, so that re-optimizing from the actual state (closed loop) can differ from – and do better than – blindly following the original plan (open loop). Without disturbances, the state at every stage evolves exactly as predicted when the open-loop plan was computed, so the “actual” state the closed-loop policy would react to is, at every stage, precisely the state the open-loop plan already assumed. The closed-loop policy therefore prescribes the same action as the open-loop plan at every stage, the two strategies coincide exactly, and so do their costs.

3 Dynamic programming equations

3.1 A more general formalism

The transition-diagram picture used so far is intuitive, but it is useful to also express the dynamic programming algorithm in a more compact, purely algebraic formalism – equivalent to the one above, but easier to generalize to the settings encountered later in the course (stochastic disturbances, continuous state spaces, and so on).

As before, the dynamic model is \(x_{k+1}=f_k(x_k,u_k)\) for \(k\in\{0,\dots,h-1\}\), and the cost is \(\sum_{k=0}^{h-1} g_k(x_k,u_k)+g_h(x_h)\).

Concretely, at each stage \(k\) the state takes values in a finite set, \(x_0\in\{1,\dots,n_0\}\), \(x_1\in\{1,\dots,n_1\}\), …, \(x_h\in\{1,\dots,n_h\}\), and the action available at state \(x_k\) takes values in a set that may depend on the state, \(u_0\in\{1,\dots,m_{0,x_0}\}\), \(u_1\in\{1,\dots,m_{1,x_1}\}\), …. The stage costs are simply the arrow costs from before, relabeled as \(g_0(x_0,u_0)=c^0_{x_0,u_0}\), \(g_1(x_1,u_1)=c^1_{x_1,u_1}\), …, and the terminal cost as \(g_h(x_h)=c^h_{x_h}\).

3.2 The dynamic programming equations

In this formalism, the dynamic programming algorithm becomes a compact pair of recursive equations. Denoting by \(U_k(i)\) the set of actions available at state \(i\) and stage \(k\), we initialize \[J_h(i) = g_h(i)\] for every state \(i\), and then, for each stage starting from the last and moving backward, compute \[ J_k(i) = \min_{j\in U_k(i)} \; g_k(i,j) + J_{k+1}(j), \] together with the corresponding minimizer \(\mu_k(i)=j^\star\). The function \(J_k\) is exactly the cost-to-go at stage \(k\) from the diagram picture above, and \(\mu_k\) is exactly the optimal policy: at each state \(i\) and stage \(k\), the policy prescribes the action \(\mu_k(i)\) that achieves the minimum in the recursion. These two equations – the initialization at the terminal stage and the backward recursion – are what we will refer to as the dynamic programming equations throughout the rest of the course; the same two equations, suitably adapted, will reappear for stochastic, continuous-state, and infinite-horizon problems.

The dynamic programming equations express a trade-off often encountered in real life: should a decision be made to maximize immediate reward (the function \(g_k\)), or should it instead favor the long-run reward (the function \(J_{k+1}\))? The dynamic programming equations naturally strike this balance, by adding the two together and optimizing their sum at every stage, rather than treating either one in isolation.

NoteSolution

At each of the \(k\in\{0,\dots,4\}\) stages (that is, \(h=5\) stages), each of the \(4\) states has \(3\) available actions, so \(4\times 3 = 12\) cost values are needed per stage. 3.3 Historical note

Dynamic programming was proposed in the 1940s by the American mathematician Richard E. Bellman, whose name is now attached to the recursive equations above (the “Bellman equations”) and to the principle of optimality itself.

Photo: via Wikipedia.

Bellman later reflected on the choice to pursue this line of research in these terms:

“I was intrigued by dynamic programming. It was clear to me that there was a good deal of good analysis there. Furthermore, I could see many applications. It was a clear choice. I could either be a traditional intellectual, or a modern intellectual using the results of my research for the problems of contemporary society.”

– R. E. Bellman (1920-1984)

4 Summary

This unit introduced optimal control as the problem of determining several optimal decisions over time, each allowed to depend on the state of the system, in contrast with static optimization’s single decision. We distinguished three broad classes of optimal control problems – discrete optimization, stage-decision, and continuous-time control – and surveyed a wide range of applications across engineering, operations research, computer science, and reinforcement learning. We then introduced the dynamic programming algorithm: a backward recursion, grounded in the principle of optimality, that computes optimal decisions stage by stage and yields both the optimal policy and the optimal cost-to-go at every state and stage. The same recursion, expressed compactly as the dynamic programming equations, will be extended throughout the rest of the course to stochastic, continuous, and infinite-horizon settings.
