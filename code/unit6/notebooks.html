1 Linear quadratic control with stochastic disturbances

The first half of the unit, made concrete: the finite-horizon LQ controller applied to a plant driven by random disturbances. Two things are worth watching. The controller copes with the disturbances without any modification – this is certainty equivalence, the optimal gains being the deterministic ones – and the expected cost predicted by the theory, \(x_0^\intercal P_0x_0+\sum_k\text{trace}(P_{k+1}W)\), matches the average cost over Monte Carlo runs. That second check is the one to run yourself: it turns an identity on the page into a number.

Download A_6_LQCstochastic.ipynb

2 The Kalman filter

The filter itself, iterated on a worked example for long enough that the gains converge to their stationary values, and then checked against the library routine. Running it is the quickest way to see the point made in the notes: the covariances \(\Phi_{k|k-1}\), \(\Phi_{k|k}\) and the gain \(L_k\) do not depend on the measurements at all, so they can be computed offline before any data arrives.

Download A_6_Kalman.ipynb

3 LQG control of an inverted pendulum

The separation principle at work on the inverted pendulum of Unit 5, which here must be controlled from a single noisy measurement rather than the full state. The average-cost infinite-horizon LQG controller is assembled from the two dual Riccati equations – the LQR gain and the stationary Kalman gain, designed independently – and the comparison against Unit 5’s full-state response shows exactly what partial information costs.

Download A_6_lqg.ipynb

4 Sensor fusion: lidar and accelerometer on a mass-spring-damper

An object on a spring carries two sensors: a lidar measuring distance to a wall, and an accelerometer. Neither alone gives a good position estimate – the lidar is noisy, the accelerometer drifts when integrated twice – but the Kalman filter merges them into one estimate that is better than either, and an LQG controller is then built on top. This is sensor fusion in its most recognizable form, and the clearest argument for why the filter is worth the algebra.

Download A_6_K_SensorFusionAndLQG.ipynb

5 IMU pose estimation: Kalman versus complementary filter

An inertial measurement unit combines gyroscopes, accelerometers and magnetometers: the gyroscope measures angular velocity, while the accelerometer and magnetometer together yield the attitude angles. With the kinematics linearized the three angles decouple, so each is a small estimation problem. The notebook compares the Kalman filter against the complementary filter, the fixed-gain scheme widely used on embedded hardware, which is the practical question an engineer actually faces: is the extra machinery worth it here?

Download A_6_K_KalmanIMU.ipynb

6 A min-max game against a worst-case disturbance

The same linear plant and quadratic cost as the rest of the unit, but with the disturbance \(\omega_k\) no longer a sequence of independent Gaussian variables: here it is chosen adversarially, to make the cost as large as the controller tries to make it small. The problem becomes a min-max game rather than a minimization, and the question is what survives of the Riccati machinery when the uncertainty stops being random and starts pushing back.
