1 REINFORCE on the continuous mountain car

The Monte-Carlo policy gradient method of the unit, on the example from slide 19. A car sits in a valley with an engine too weak to climb straight out; the action is a continuous acceleration, which is the reason this example belongs here rather than in Unit 12 – a Q-factor method would have to minimise over a continuous set at every step, while a policy gradient method simply outputs the action.

The policy is a Gaussian: the network maps the state to a mean and a log standard deviation, and the action is drawn from that distribution. This is the concrete form of the unit’s requirement that the policy be stochastic, so that the cost is differentiable in the parameters.

Worth knowing before running it: this does not solve the problem, and the reason is the interesting part. Acting costs \(-0.1u^2\) per step while the \(+100\) arrives only at the flag, so until the car has reached the flag once the gradient sees nothing but wasted fuel. The policy improves by shrinking its actions towards zero, and because the Gaussian’s width is itself a learned parameter, exploration shuts down with it. The notebook measures the collapse – the standard deviation ends at \(0.0057\), from about \(1.0\) – and includes an optional rerun with the width held open, which is the cheapest of the standard remedies.

The notebook also names something the script does quietly: normalising the returns before forming the loss is the baseline subtraction of the deck’s slide 13, so the script’s “REINFORCE” is already REINFORCE with a crude constant baseline.

2 Actor-critic on the continuous mountain car

The same problem with a critic added, which is the deck’s slide 17. The two networks are the actor \(\pi(u\mid x,\theta)\) and the critic \(\hat J(x,\alpha)\), and the quantity passed between them is the temporal difference of Unit 11: the critic fits it, and the actor uses it as the signed weight on its gradient.

The unit’s claim is that this reduces the variance of the policy gradient, and the notebook measures it rather than repeating it. Along the same trajectories under one fixed policy it collects the two quantities that multiply \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi\) – the Monte-Carlo return \(G_k\) for REINFORCE, the TD error \(\delta_k\) for actor-critic – and compares their spreads, with histograms. The structural reason is visible in the definitions: \(G_k\) accumulates every reward to the end of the episode, so its spread grows with the horizon, while \(\delta_k\) involves one reward and two critic evaluations, so it does not. In the runs recorded in README.md the ratio is about \(109\).

The honest ending is worth the detour. The critic reduces the variance of the gradient estimate; it does not change what the gradient points at. On this environment that means the actor-critic reaches the same “do nothing” local optimum as REINFORCE – and reaches it faster, after one episode rather than twenty-nine, precisely because its estimates are less noisy. Variance reduction makes a method commit to the direction it is being pushed, which is exactly what you want when the direction is right.
