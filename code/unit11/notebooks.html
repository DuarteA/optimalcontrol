1 Stochastic inventory control by policy iteration

The inventory problem of Unit 2, solved a third way. Unit 2 used stochastic dynamic programming, code/unit10/ learned it by Q-learning, and here it is solved by policy iteration: evaluate the current policy, improve it greedily, repeat until the improvement step hands back the policy it was given.

The notebook reproduces the lecture’s lattice sequence number for number. The cost at stock \(0\), stage \(0\) after each improvement step is \(0\) for the do-nothing policy \(\mu^0\), then \(-25.30\), \(-25.70\), \(-25.75\) and finally \(-25.95\), with \(\mu^5=\mu^4\) ending the loop – one lattice diagram per number. The optimal stage-0 column \(-25.95,\,-30.75,\,-36.05,\,-40.65,\,-45.45\) agrees to the last digit with Unit 2’s stochastic DP and with the exact reference inside Unit 10’s Q-learning notebook. Three independent routes to the same five numbers is the real content here.

Policy iteration takes five evaluations where DP takes one backward sweep, so on this finite-horizon problem it is more work. Its advantage belongs to the infinite-horizon case, where DP needs many value-iteration sweeps while policy evaluation is a single linear solve.

2 State and input discretization by policy iteration

The minimum-energy problem of Unit 7 again: drive a double integrator from rest at the origin to position \(1\) with zero velocity, minimising \(\int_0^1u^2\,dt\). Unit 7 discretized state, input and time and solved the grid problem by backward dynamic programming; here the same grid problem goes through policy iteration, and the notebook runs both side by side.

The point is that they agree exactly – J[0] = 801.0 either way, policy \(u=[4,-4]\), positions \([0,\,0.5,\,1]\), which is also the trajectory Unit 10’s Q-learning notebook recovered without a model. On a problem this size the agreement can be checked rather than argued.

The cost reads \(16.02\) here against Unit 7’s \(16.00\), and the notebook explains the two hundredths rather than leaving them hanging: the terminal cost is \(1\) at the target and \(+\infty\) elsewhere, where the \(1\) is a marker saying “this state is admissible” rather than a real cost, and it contributes \(dt\cdot1\cdot du^2=0.02\).

3 Stopping time: policy evaluation by simulation

The asset-selling problem of Unit 10, asking a different question. Unit 10 looked for the optimal threshold; here the threshold is fixed – sell whenever the offer clears \(c_{25}=12\,400\) – and the notebook evaluates what that policy is worth, from simulated offers alone.

This is the policy-evaluation half of policy iteration in the smallest setting where it can be seen. With the policy fixed there is no minimisation anywhere, so the value of waiting satisfies a linear relation and has a closed form. Stochastic approximation gives \(15\,152.3\) against the closed form \(15\,163.4\): a \(0.07\%\) error, which is the whole argument for the method in one number.

Two things are worth drawing out. The fixed policy is worth about \(15\,160\) while Unit 10’s optimal threshold of \(16\,900\) is worth \(16\,858\) – evaluating a policy and improving it are genuinely separate operations, and this notebook does only the first. And the closed form exists only because the policy is fixed; the moment a \(\min\) enters, the relation stops being linear and no such formula survives. That is exactly why policy evaluation is the cheap half.

4 Stochastic inventory control, simulation-based

The first notebook ran policy iteration with the model in hand. Here the same loop runs without one: the evaluation step becomes simulated trajectories. The inner update bootstraps on \(Q(x_{k+1},\mu(x_{k+1}))\) – the value of the action the current policy would take, not a minimum – which is what makes it SARSA-style policy evaluation rather than Q-learning. The outer loop then improves greedily. Neither step is exact; the hope is that the pair still converges.

Because the exact answer sits in the notebook next door, every entry can be checked. Stage 0 comes out accurate to a few tenths and the final stage is exact, its Q-factors coming straight from the salvage value with no bootstrapping.

The entries still sitting at zero are the instructive ones. High stock at stages 1 and 2 is never visited: trajectories start at stage 0 and the policy orders down, so reaching a stock of \(3\) or \(4\) mid-horizon would take a large order into an already-full shop, and the exploration rate has dropped to \(\epsilon/12\) by then so chance stops doing it either. That is the convergence theorem’s first condition – every state-action pair infinitely often – visibly not holding, and it is the same failure Unit 10’s Q-learning notebook shows. Here it costs nothing, because those states are unreachable under any good policy. Which is the point the unit’s exploration slide makes with this very example.

5 Robot path planning, simulation-based

The noisy \(13\times14\) grid of Units 2 and 3, solved by generalized policy iteration from simulation and compared against the exact stochastic DP computed in the same notebook.

It also measures a bug in the original script, which is the more useful half. Exploration draws from sampledist(ones(1,nu(x(k)))) – an unnormalised vector of ones, so the cumulative sum is \([1,2,3]\), the uniform draw is always below \(1\), and the “random” exploratory action is always the first one. Exploration is not uniform over up, straight and down; it is “always try up”. The notebook runs it both ways: the maximum error against the exact cost-to-go is \(6.39\) as written and \(1.89\) with the one-character fix, and the middle rows are the ones overestimated, because the robot never discovers that going down is sometimes right.

Worth sitting with, because the algorithm, the step size and the number of simulations are all fine. The answer is wrong because one branch of the action space was never tried.

6 Frozen lake: Q-learning vs SARSA

The \(4\times4\) slippery frozen lake, with the two algorithms side by side. One line separates them: Q-learning’s target uses the best action at the next state, SARSA’s uses the action the \(\epsilon\)-greedy policy actually takes – off-policy against on-policy, in a single expression. Both reach a win rate near \(0.5\) and settle on similar policies, which is the point the lecture makes. The notebook also animates one episode under the initial random policy and under the learned one.

Worth looking at the arrow grids at the end: several point straight into a wall. On a slippery lake that is correct – commanding into a wall makes the intended move a no-op and leaves only the two perpendicular slips, which is how you move sideways with no chance of sliding into a hole.

7 Blackjack: on-policy vs off-policy evaluation

Blackjack is small enough to solve exactly by value iteration, so every simulation-based estimate can be checked against the truth – which is what makes it the right example for off-policy learning. The notebook computes the optimal policy exactly, then estimates one Q-factor, \(Q\big((20,10,\text{False}),\text{stick}\big)\), three ways: on-policy, and off-policy with ordinary and with weighted importance sampling, using an \(\epsilon\)-soft behaviour policy with \(\epsilon=0.6\).

The exact answer is \(+0.4350\), and all three estimators land on it. The difference between the two off-policy estimators is variance, not location, and the rolling-variance panel shows the price ordinary importance sampling pays for being unbiased.
