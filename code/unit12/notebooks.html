1 Fitting a neural network by least squares

The picture behind the unit’s claim that training an approximation architecture is nothing more than a regression problem. A scalar function is sampled with noise, a small ReLU network is fitted to the samples by minimising the mean squared error, and the fit is compared against the function the samples came from.

Two things are worth looking at in the result. The fit is good in the middle and flattens at the edges of the domain, because a ReLU network is piecewise linear and extrapolates by continuing its last segment. And it passes through the middle of the cloud of noisy points rather than through the points, which is exactly what the regression is asking for: the minimiser of the expected squared error is \(\mathbb{E}[J\mid x]\).

The notebook prints the noise floor alongside the training loss – the mean squared error that even a perfect fit would leave against noisy targets. That turns the loss from a number into a diagnostic.

2 Stochastic gradient against the full gradient

The same regression run twice from identical initial weights, once with minibatches and once with the full gradient. This is the measurement behind the table on the deck’s slide 11, and it turns out to say something slightly different from what the slide says.

The losses reproduce exactly – the full-gradient run lands on \(0.011145\), the slide’s figure. But it only does so after \(120\,000\) epochs, not the \(12\,000\) the slide prints: the script computes epochs*300, and at \(12\,000\) the loss is still \(0.2796\). The notebook runs both so the point is not a matter of opinion.

More interesting, the comparison as written varies the optimiser as well as the batch size – Adam for the stochastic run, plain SGD for the full one. Separating them, by running the full gradient with Adam at an equal number of parameter updates, shows that on a problem with \(255\) training points the full gradient is not the expensive option at all. The case for stochastic gradient is about large \(N\), and the lunar-lander notebooks are where that bites: there the data set is a replay buffer of \(50\,000\) transitions.

3 Deep Q-learning on the lunar lander

Unit 10’s Q-learning with the table thrown away. The state is an eight-dimensional continuous vector, so there is nothing to tabulate: the Q-factors are a neural network and the Bellman error is the regression target of the first two notebooks.

What is new are the two devices on the deck’s “special features” slide – an experience replay buffer and a slowly updated target network – and the notebook does not just describe them. It reruns the agent with the replay buffer shrunk to a single minibatch, and again with the target network copied every step, so that what they are for is visible rather than asserted. Both exist to stop the regression target from moving in step with the estimate being fitted, which is precisely the assumption the tabular convergence proof of Unit 10 relied on and that a stream of consecutive transitions violates.

The notebook also saves the animation of the learned policy that the deck links to.

4 Deep Q-learning against SARSA with function approximation

Unit 11 put Q-learning and SARSA side by side in the tabular case and found they differ in exactly one symbol. This notebook does the same comparison once both have had their tables replaced by a network.

They perform similarly, which is the deck’s claim, and the notebook gives the reason rather than only the picture: with \(\epsilon\) decayed to \(0.02\), the action SARSA bootstraps on is the maximising action \(98.5\%\) of the time, so the two targets agree on all but one step in seventy. Raising the exploration floor to \(0.2\) drops that to \(85\%\) and the two begin to separate.

It also records why SARSA has no replay buffer. This is not a simplification: a stored transition carries the action an older policy chose, while SARSA’s target needs the action the current policy would take. That is Problem 11.4’s observation that the data table is one column short, restated as a property of the algorithm rather than of the exercise.
